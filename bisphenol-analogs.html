The compound

Bisphenol A was restricted, not retired.

BPA is a plastic additive with endocrine-disrupting properties. That was enough to get it banned from certain products — not enough to remove the need for a chemical that does its job.

The substitutes

It was replaced by molecules that look almost exactly like it.

BPE, BPG, BPAP and BPZ all keep the same two phenol rings. Only the bridge between them changes. Substitutes designed this way may be equally, or even more, bioactive.

Why the bridge matters

Every substitution makes the molecule more hydrophobic.

BPA sits at logP 3.3 — the lowest of the set. Every one of its four replacements is more hydrophobic, up to 6.3. And hydrophobicity is what drives baseline, narcotic toxicity.

The reference

Measured against two known baseline toxicants.

Diphenylamine and 4-methylaniline are presumed to act by narcosis alone. They set the line separating “toxic because it is hydrophobic” from “toxic through a specific mechanism”.

The result

Effects sat within the range of predicted baseline toxicity.

For every bisphenol tested. And the most sensitive endpoint across all of them was one that is easy to miss by eye: the posterior swim bladder simply failing to appear.

The regrettable substitution problem

When a chemical is restricted, the pressure is to replace it quickly with something structurally close enough to do the same job. The replacement inherits the properties that made the original useful — and often the ones that made it a problem. The question is whether anyone checks before it is in production.

BPA was banned from certain products specifically because of its endocrine-disrupting properties. The substitutes that followed — bisphenol E, G, AP and Z — were not assessed to the same depth. This study asked a narrower and more tractable question than “are they safe”: across a set of non-endocrine, (neuro)developmental endpoints, do these analogues do anything their hydrophobicity alone would not predict?

How it was measured

A high-content screening assay in zebrafish (Danio rerio) embryos, built on automated quantitative image and video analysis rather than scoring by eye. That choice is the methodological core of the study: it removes observer bias and makes the results reproducible in a way manual phenotype scoring is not.

Exposure design

Five concentrations per bisphenol. The highest was set at predicted baseline toxicity, then a two-fold serial dilution below it.

Endpoints

Behaviour, heart rate, and quantified deviation of morphological endpoints from controls.

Modelling

Concentration–response modelling on every endpoint to derive effect concentrations.

Specificity

Effect ratios against observed mortality, and comparison to two presumed baseline toxicants.

What came out of it

The posterior swim bladder was the most sensitive endpoint

Across every bisphenol tested, absence of the posterior swim bladder responded at lower concentrations than any other endpoint — and it was most sensitive of all for bisphenol E. It is also exactly the kind of endpoint automated imaging catches and a human scorer, working quickly, does not.

The effects were consistent with baseline toxicity

Compared against predicted baseline toxicity, the developmental effects of all five bisphenols fell within the range expected from hydrophobicity alone. On these (neuro)developmental endpoints, none of the substitutes showed potency beyond narcosis.

A screening route for the analogues nobody has tested yet

There are far more BPA analogues than there is capacity to characterise in detail. The detailed assessment of BPA and its four substitutes was used to build a screening approach for prioritising which of the remaining analogues deserve that depth of work.
